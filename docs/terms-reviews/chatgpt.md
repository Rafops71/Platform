2. Section 11 – Limitation of liability

This is where I disagree most with Grok’s suggested approach.

Your existing wording:

To the fullest extent permitted by law, neither the Platform nor the Operators are liable for any loss or damage of any kind arising out of or in connection with your use of the Platform or with any dealing between Participants.

The intention is understandable.

You want to establish that:

* You are not responsible for the buyer.
* You are not responsible for the seller.
* You do not verify documents.
* You do not guarantee commodities.
* You do not guarantee transactions.
* You are not responsible if someone commits fraud.
* You are not responsible if a transaction collapses.

That is sensible.

However, there is an important distinction.

You cannot simply declare yourself exempt from every possible form of liability.

Under the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977:

* Section 2 addresses exclusions of negligence liability.
* Section 3 addresses certain contractual exclusions, including contracts made on written standard terms.
* Section 11 establishes the reasonableness test.

The Misrepresentation Act 1967, section 3, also subjects exclusions of misrepresentation liability to reasonableness.

Sources:

* UCTA 1977 – Sections 2, 3 and 11⁠￼
* Misrepresentation Act 1967 – Section 3⁠￼

My recommendation

I would introduce a financial limitation of liability, but I would NOT automatically adopt Grok’s suggestion of limiting liability to commissions received during the preceding 12 months.

Why?

Imagine:

* You have just started operating.
* You have received no commission.
* Someone brings a claim against you.

Your proposed liability cap could effectively be zero.

That creates an additional enforceability argument.

The cap should be commercially defensible and proportionate to the nature of the service, the risks, the insurance position and the bargaining circumstances.

Suggested wording for your solicitor

This is a drafting starting point, not a declaration that the wording will necessarily satisfy UCTA.

11. Limitation of liability

3. Section 8 – Commission and non-circumvention

This is arguably the most commercially important section of your entire document.

Your business model depends on it.

Grok considers it generally strong. I agree with that assessment, but I see some areas requiring clarification.

A. Commission agreement

Your existing wording:

The commission, its basis (a percentage of the transaction value, or a fixed sum per unit in the currency of the transaction) and which party pays it are agreed in writing between the Operators and the paying party during the negotiation, before the introduction is made.

This is good.

It establishes three important things:

1. The commission is agreed before introduction.
2. The basis of calculation is established.
3. The paying party is identified.

However, I would make one additional point explicit.

The commission agreement must identify the transaction or opportunity to which it applies.

Otherwise, disagreements can arise about whether subsequent transactions, additional quantities or repeat purchases are covered.

B. Twenty-four-month protection

I would retain the 24-month period.

However, I would ask your solicitor to examine precisely when the period starts and what happens when:

* The original transaction is concluded after 24 months.
* The parties negotiate several shipments.
* The original buyer purchases additional quantities.
* A transaction is concluded through an affiliate.
* A participant introduces another intermediary.

The current wording is broad, but broad wording does not automatically mean enforceable wording.

C. Commission survives termination

I agree with Grok here.

I would expressly establish that termination of membership does not terminate commission obligations arising from previous introductions.

Suggested addition:

Termination, suspension or withdrawal of a Participant’s membership or access to the Platform shall not affect any commission obligation arising from an introduction made before such termination, suspension or withdrawal. All such obligations shall survive and remain enforceable in accordance with this section.

D. One additional concern

Your document says:

The commission is payable whenever a transaction results from an introduction made through the Platform.

But it does not clearly establish the precise event triggering payment.

For example:

* Is commission payable upon signing the sales contract?
* Upon payment?
* Upon delivery?
* Proportionally against successive shipments?

These are commercially important distinctions.

I would leave the actual payment trigger to the individual commission agreement, which should be signed separately.

4. Dual agency – something Grok under-emphasised

This is particularly important because you explicitly state that the Operators may act for both sides.

Your Section 8 says:

You acknowledge that the Operators may act for, and receive commission from, both sides of the same transaction.

That is useful disclosure.

However, there is a distinction between disclosing that you receive commission and obtaining informed consent to the conflicts of interest that can arise from acting for both parties.

Under English law, fiduciary duties can arise from an agency relationship.

A contractual statement that you are an intermediary does not automatically eliminate those duties.

Two relevant Court of Appeal decisions are:

* Wilson v Hurstanger Ltd [2007] EWCA Civ 299.
* Johnson v FirstRand Bank Ltd [2024] EWCA Civ 1282.

Both illustrate the importance of informed consent to commission arrangements and conflicts of interest. 

My recommendation

Ask your solicitor to consider whether Section 8 should expressly explain:

* That you may represent or act for both sides.
* That your financial interests may not always be identical to those of either Participant.
* That each Participant expressly consents to this arrangement.
* That the commission arrangements are disclosed to the relevant paying parties.
* That the Operators are not undertaking to negotiate the best possible commercial terms for either party.

I would not simply add a generic sentence saying that no fiduciary duties exist.

Whether such duties arise depends on the actual relationship and conduct, not merely the terminology used in the contract.

This is an important issue to resolve before launch.

5. Section 15 – English law and DIFC Courts

This deserves a more precise examination than Grok provided.

Your clause establishes:

* Governing law: England and Wales.
* Courts: Dubai International Financial Centre Courts.
* Written agreement to DIFC jurisdiction.

These are not inherently incompatible.

You can choose English substantive law while agreeing to have disputes heard by the DIFC Courts.

Importantly, Article 23 of Dubai Law No. 2 of 2025 expressly recognises that the DIFC Courts may apply another law where the parties have expressly agreed to it.

Article 14(B) provides for jurisdiction where parties expressly agree in writing through specific, clear and express provisions.

Your clause expressly identifies Article 14(B).

I checked the actual legislation.

Dubai Law No. 2 of 2025 – Official legislation⁠￼

What I would change

I would not fundamentally change the structure.

However, there are three points your solicitor should examine.

1. Scope

Your clause governs disputes arising from the Terms and Conditions.

It does not necessarily govern disputes arising from separate commodity sale contracts between Participants.

Those contracts need their own governing law and jurisdiction provisions.

2. International enforcement

A DIFC judgment is not automatically equivalent to having an enforceable judgment in every country where a Participant or its assets might be located.

Enforcement arrangements need to be considered.

3. Service of proceedings

Your clause permits service by email and post.

Your solicitor should verify that the wording operates consistently with the DIFC Courts’ procedural rules, including service outside the jurisdiction.

My conclusion

I see no fundamental problem with choosing English law and DIFC Courts.

I would retain the structure, subject to confirmation by your solicitor.

6. Section 3 – An important weakness concerning future companies

Your current wording says:

If you accept these Terms as an individual, your obligations under them continue to bind you, and extend to any company or other entity you later form, control or trade through…

I would be careful here.

An individual cannot automatically impose contractual obligations on a separate company simply by declaring that the obligations extend to that company.

The company is a separate legal person.

Your wording may establish personal obligations concerning the individual’s future conduct, but it does not automatically make a future company a party to the agreement.

Recommendation

Ask your solicitor to consider a provision requiring the Participant to procure that any relevant company or entity through which the opportunity is pursued:

* Accepts the applicable Terms.
* Signs an appropriate accession or undertaking.
* Accepts the relevant commission arrangements.

This is particularly important for your non-circumvention protection.

7. Section 10 – Privacy Notice

Grok correctly identified this as a high-priority issue.

However, I would go further.

The problem is not simply the missing placeholders.

The Privacy Notice currently describes what you collect and why, but it does not adequately identify the lawful basis for each processing activity.

UK GDPR Article 13 requires information including:

* The identity of the controller.
* The purposes of processing.
* The lawful basis.
* Legitimate interests, where applicable.
* Recipients.
* International transfers and safeguards.
* Data retention.
* Individual rights.

The ICO’s guidance confirms that privacy information must be provided when personal data is collected. 

Particular concerns

A. Lawful basis

You should identify whether processing is based on:

* Contractual necessity.
* Legitimate interests.
* Legal obligations.

These bases should be assessed separately for different processing activities.

B. International transfers

You mention Supabase, Resend and GitHub.

You need to establish the actual hosting and processing locations, the relevant contractual arrangements and the applicable transfer mechanisms.

Do not assume that a provider being established in the United States automatically means every processing activity constitutes a restricted transfer. The actual arrangements matter.

The ICO updated its international transfer guidance in January 2026. 

C. Retention

Your wording:

Data is retained only for as long as it is needed…

This is rather general.

I would establish actual retention periods or meaningful criteria for the different categories of data.

D. Data subject rights

You should explicitly explain the applicable rights, including access, rectification, erasure, restriction, objection and portability where applicable.

E. Controller identity

You identify Rafael Efraim personally.

That is consistent with the current sole-operator arrangement, assuming you are indeed the controller.

When the company is incorporated, the privacy notice must be updated if the controller changes.

One additional point: depending on where you operate and whom you target, UK GDPR, EU GDPR and potentially other privacy regimes may apply. Your solicitor should establish the actual territorial scope.
